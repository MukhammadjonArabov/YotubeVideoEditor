# Fact-check: Why Is Your Brain Addicted to BAD News?

Every fact scene, its source, how it was checked and how the wording was kept honest. Checked 2026-10-27.

## Method
- Every study was found and its abstract or a reliable summary opened; contested findings are told as contested.
- No mental-health advice: the 'what to do' part only describes what studies report, and says 'this isn't advice'.

## Sources and checks

### Robertson et al. (2023). Negativity drives online news consumption. Nature Human Behaviour 7, 812-822
- How verified: Searched 2026-10-27; LMU press release, Nieman Lab, PsyPost and the paper PDF hosted by LMU: about 105,000 Upworthy headline variants, 22,743 randomised tests, about 5.7 million clicks from more than 370 million impressions; each extra negative word raised click-through by 2.3% for an average-length headline; positive words lowered it; sad words worked more than angry or scary ones.
- Wording safeguard: 'About two percent per negative word', 'one big site', not 'all news'.

### Soroka, Fournier & Nir (2019). Cross-national evidence of a negativity bias in psychophysiological reactions to news. PNAS 116, 18888-18892
- How verified: Searched 2026-10-27; PMC full text (PMC6754543), PsyPost: 1,156 people in 17 countries watched BBC World News clips with skin-conductance and pulse sensors; negative stories drew stronger reactions on average; not significant in every country, big differences between individuals; the authors say there is a large audience for positive news.
- Wording safeguard: 'On average', 'not in every country', 'people differ a lot' said out loud.

### Baumeister, Bratslavsky, Finkenauer & Vohs (2001). Bad is stronger than good. Review of General Psychology 5, 323-370
- How verified: Searched 2026-10-27; the paper PDF hosted at UNC (abstract and opening): bad events have stronger effects across everyday events, major life events, close relationships and learning; hardly any exceptions found. The Gottman 5-to-1 link is only from secondary sources.
- Wording safeguard: Called a big review; the 5-to-1 ratio is not used as a number.

### Öhman, Flykt & Esteves (2001). Emotion drives attention: Detecting the snake in the grass. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General 130, 466-478
- How verified: Searched 2026-10-27; abstract via Universidade Católica record and later reviews: snakes and spiders found faster among flowers and mushrooms than the reverse; search time for threats less affected by the number of distractors; a 2012 review says snakes are detected faster in demanding displays.
- Wording safeguard: 'Found faster' only; no millisecond numbers.

### Nesse (2001). The smoke detector principle. Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences 935, 75-85; Nesse (2019), Evolution, Medicine, and Public Health
- How verified: Searched 2026-10-27; University of Michigan Deep Blue record and Nesse's own page: when a defense is cheap and the harm it prevents is large, the best system gives many false alarms (signal detection logic).
- Wording safeguard: Explained as an idea from evolutionary medicine, not a measured fact.

### Ito, Larsen, Smith & Cacioppo (1998). Negative information weighs more heavily on the brain. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 75, 887-900
- How verified: Searched 2026-10-27; PubMed 9825526 abstract: late positive potentials larger for negative than positive pictures matched for probability, extremity and arousal. Later ERP studies are mixed (PMC4084413 review).
- Wording safeguard: 'In this study', and 'later studies are mixed' is said.

### Vaish, Grossmann & Woodward (2008). Not all emotions are created equal: the negativity bias in social-emotional development. Psychological Bulletin 134, 383-403
- How verified: Searched 2026-10-27; Birkbeck repository and PMC3652533 abstract: a tendency to attend to, learn from and use negative information more than positive; the paper reviews when it appears in development (infants by about the end of the first year).
- Wording safeguard: Only the review's main claim; no infant percentages.

### Brown, Imai, Vieider & Camerer (2021/2024), meta-analysis of loss aversion (lambda about 1.8-2.1); Gal & Rucker (2018), The loss of loss aversion, Journal of Consumer Psychology
- How verified: Searched 2026-10-27; Judgment and Decision Making 2022 discussion and Social Science Space: meta-analysis gives lambda about 1.8-2.1 (losses weigh about twice as much); Gal & Rucker argue it is overgeneralised, especially for small stakes; critics do not claim there is no asymmetry.
- Wording safeguard: 'About twice, in many studies', the dispute stated.

### Mather & Carstensen (2005). Aging and motivated cognition: the positivity effect in attention and memory. Trends in Cognitive Sciences 9, 496-502; Reed & Carstensen (2012), Frontiers in Psychology
- How verified: Searched 2026-10-27; Mather & Carstensen 2005 PDF and Reed & Carstensen 2012 (PMC3459016): older adults attend to and remember positive more than negative relative to younger adults; called an 'effect' because it is a relative shift; some studies find no age difference.
- Wording safeguard: 'Relative to younger people', 'not every study finds it'.

### Wood & Kisley (2006), Psychology and Aging; Kisley, Wood & Burrows (2007), Psychological Science (older adults show no ERP negativity bias)
- How verified: Searched 2026-10-27; summarised in PMC4084413: Wood & Kisley 2006 found the ERP negativity bias in younger but not older adults; Kisley et al. 2007 traced it to smaller responses to negative images with age.
- Wording safeguard: 'In brain-wave studies', 'shrinks' rather than 'disappears for everyone'.

## Claims softened or dropped
- Gottman's '5 to 1' ratio: only in secondary sources, dropped.
- Hamlin et al. (2007) infant helper/hinderer study: replication failures (ManyBabies), dropped.
- 'Losses hurt exactly twice as much': told as 'about twice in many studies', with Gal & Rucker's critique.
- Brain-wave negativity bias: 'in this study', 'later studies are mixed'.
- Positivity effect: 'relative to younger people', 'not every study finds it'.
- Nesse 2005 Evolution and Human Behavior citation could not be confirmed; the 2001 Annals paper is cited instead.
