# Fact-check: Why Do Humans LAUGH?

Every fact scene, its source, how it was checked and how the wording was kept honest. Checked 2026-11-08.

## Method
- Every study was found online and its abstract, record or a reliable summary opened; contested points are told as such.
- No health advice: laughter is never called a cure or treatment; the pain study is told as a link with an unmeasured mechanism.
- The 1962 epidemic is told with respect for the people involved; no mockery.

## Sources and checks

### Blakemore, Wolpert & Frith (1998). Central cancellation of self-produced tickle sensation. Nature Neuroscience 1, 635-640
- How verified: Searched 2026-11-08; paper copies and Scientific American explainer: the cerebellum predicts the touch your own movement makes and damps the brain's response, so self-tickling feels weaker; a delay makes it tickle more.
- Wording safeguard: 'The brain predicts it', no exact numbers.

### McDonald (2008). The world's oldest jokes, University of Wolverhampton (Sumerian proverb, about 1900 BCE; Westcar Papyrus riddle)
- How verified: Searched 2026-11-08; University of Wolverhampton news release (Aug 2008), Guinness World Records, Chortle: Sumerian proverb about a young woman breaking wind in her husband's lap, dated to at least 1900 BCE, possibly older; second on the list an Egyptian riddle from the Westcar Papyrus (1800-1600 BCE).
- Wording safeguard: 'About four thousand years old', 'one of the oldest recorded jokes'; joke told gently.

### Cai, Chen, White & Scott (2019). Modulation of humor ratings of bad jokes by other people's laughter. Current Biology 29, R677-R678
- How verified: Searched 2026-11-08; ScienceDaily, NPR and Neuroscience News on the Current Biology paper: 40 weak 'dad jokes', 72 adults; added laughter raised funniness ratings, spontaneous laughter more (about 15-20%) than posed laughter (about 10%).
- Wording safeguard: 'A little funnier', 'real laughter more than fake'; percentages not overstated.

### Rankin & Philip (1963). An epidemic of laughing in the Bukoba district of Tanganyika. Central African Journal of Medicine 9; Hempelmann (2007). Humor 20
- How verified: Searched 2026-11-08; several accounts based on Rankin & Philip 1963 and Hempelmann 2007: started 30 January 1962 with three girls at a mission boarding school in Kashasha; 95 of 159 pupils affected; school closed 18 March; spread to villages and other schools; about 14 schools closed and about 1,000 people affected by 1964; explained as mass psychogenic illness, Hempelmann links it to stress after independence. Enrolment is 195 in one secondary account.
- Wording safeguard: 'About ninety-five', 'about a thousand people', 'more than a dozen schools'; told respectfully, no mocking; cause told as 'researchers think'.

### Davila Ross, Owren & Zimmermann (2009). Reconstructing the evolution of laughter in great apes and humans. Current Biology 19, 1106-1111
- How verified: Searched 2026-11-08; National Geographic, ScienceDaily and phys.org on the Current Biology paper: tickling-induced sounds of young chimpanzees, bonobos, gorillas, orangutans and human babies compared; acoustic tree matched the family tree; common origin at least 10-16 million years ago; human laughter changed after our line split, about 4.5-6 million years ago.
- Wording safeguard: 'At least ten million years', 'our laugh is a changed version'; ape sounds called 'laugh-like panting'.

### Panksepp & Burgdorf (2003). 'Laughing' rats and the evolutionary antecedents of human joy? Physiology & Behavior 79, 533-547
- How verified: Searched 2026-11-08; Panksepp & Burgdorf papers and summaries: young rats tickled by hand make about 50 kHz chirps, too high for human ears; the authors read them as a sign of positive feeling; rats seek the tickling hand.
- Wording safeguard: 'Something like laughter', 'researchers argue', 'too high for us to hear'.

### Winkler & Bryant (2021). Play vocalisations and human laughter: a comparative review. Bioacoustics 30, 499-526
- How verified: Searched 2026-11-08; UCLA newsroom, NPR, Smithsonian on the Bioacoustics review: play sounds documented in at least 65 species, mostly mammals plus three birds (kea, parakeets, Australian magpies); only some are play-specific; list likely incomplete.
- Wording safeguard: 'At least sixty-five species', 'play sounds', not 'all animals laugh'.

### Provine, R. (2000). Laughter: A Scientific Investigation. Viking
- How verified: Searched 2026-11-08; Discover interview and First Things essay quoting Provine: about 30 times more laughter in company than alone (student logbooks, excluding TV); speakers laugh about 46% more than listeners; most conversational laughter does not follow jokes. No percentage for joke-laughter is used.
- Wording safeguard: 'About thirty times', 'about forty-six percent more', 'most laughs follow ordinary lines'.

### Dunbar et al. (2012). Social laughter is correlated with an elevated pain threshold. Proceedings of the Royal Society B 279, 1161-1167
- How verified: Searched 2026-11-08; paper record and abstract: six studies in lab and theatre; pain thresholds higher after laughing in groups; endorphins inferred from pain threshold, not measured.
- Wording safeguard: 'A link', 'scientists suspect endorphins'; no health advice, laughter never called a treatment.

### Bryant et al. (2016). Detecting affiliation in colaughter across 24 societies. PNAS 113, 4682-4687
- How verified: Searched 2026-11-08; PNAS abstract and NPR/Aarhus coverage: 966 listeners in 24 societies heard brief co-laughter of friends or new acquaintances; accuracy about 53-67%; friends' laughter louder, higher and more varied.
- Wording safeguard: 'Better than chance, not perfect', 'one second of laughter'.

## Claims softened or dropped
- 'People laugh about 18 times a day': no reliable source found, dropped.
- 'Only 10-20% of laughs follow jokes': no percentage confirmed; said as 'most laughs don't follow a joke'.
- Tanganyika numbers differ between accounts (159 vs 195 pupils, timeline); 'about ninety-five', 'about a thousand people' used.
- Rat 'laughter': told as 'laugh-like sounds' and the disagreement is said.
- Endorphins: inferred from pain thresholds, not measured; said as 'suspect' and 'a link, not proof'.
- Friends vs strangers: accuracy only 53-67%; said as 'not perfectly, better than chance'.
- 'Fake laughs sound different' is a general framing of the Bryant findings on spontaneous vs volitional laughter, kept non-numeric.
