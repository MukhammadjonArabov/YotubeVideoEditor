# Fact-check: Conversation Mistakes You Don't Know You're MAKING

Every fact scene, its source, how it was checked and how the wording was kept honest. Checked 2026-11-10.

## Method
- Every study was found online and its abstract, record or a reliable summary opened; limits are said out loud.
- No therapy or professional advice: everyday conversation habits only.

## Sources and checks

### Boothby, Cooney, Sandstrom & Clark (2018). The liking gap in conversations: do people like us more than we think? Psychological Science 29, 1742-1756
- How verified: Searched 2026-11-10; paper PDF (Wharton copy), Yale News, ScienceDaily: strangers in the lab, first-year dorm mates and the general public underestimated how much partners liked them; observers watching recordings judged liking accurately; Clark: people are wrapped up in their own worries.
- Wording safeguard: 'Probably', 'in these studies', no exact percentage given.

### Tamir & Mitchell (2012). Disclosing information about the self is intrinsically rewarding. PNAS 109, 8038-8043
- How verified: Searched 2026-11-10; PMC3361411: paper opens with 'people devote 30-40% of their speech' to their own experiences; self-disclosure activated mesolimbic dopamine regions; people gave up some money to talk about themselves.
- Wording safeguard: 'About a third', 'reward areas', no claim about exact payment amounts (sources differ).

### Derber, C. (1979; 2000). The Pursuit of Attention: Power and Ego in Everyday Life. Oxford University Press
- How verified: Known sociology book; the 'shift response' vs 'support response' terms come from it; widely summarised (e.g. Celeste Headlee, We Need to Talk).
- Wording safeguard: Introduced as 'one sociologist called it', not as a measured study.

### Newton, E. (1990). Stanford PhD dissertation (song-tapping study); described in Heath & Heath (2007), Made to Stick
- How verified: Searched 2026-11-10; Wikipedia 'Curse of knowledge' and several summaries agree: 120 songs tapped, listeners named 3 (2.5%), tappers predicted about half.
- Wording safeguard: 'About half', 'three out of a hundred and twenty'.

### Stivers et al. (2009). Universals and cultural variation in turn-taking in conversation. PNAS 106, 10587-10592
- How verified: Searched 2026-11-10; PubMed 19553212 abstract: 10 languages, all avoid overlap and keep silences between turns short; averages differ within about 250 ms. The exact '200 ms' is a secondary summary, so it is not said.
- Wording safeguard: 'A fraction of a second', 'researchers think we start planning our reply while the other person is still talking'.

### Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Minson & Gino (2017). It doesn't hurt to ask: question-asking increases liking. JPSP 113, 430-452
- How verified: Searched 2026-11-10; paper PDF (gwern) and APS summary: more questions, especially follow-up questions, raised liking; speed daters who asked more got more second-date yeses; people did not predict this; too many or rude questions can backfire; observers of transcripts did not show the effect.
- Wording safeguard: 'Follow-up questions', 'in speed-dating data', the caveat about too many questions is said.

### Kardas, Kumar & Epley (2022). Overly shallow? Miscalibrated expectations create a barrier to deeper conversation. JPSP 122, 367-398
- How verified: Searched 2026-11-10; EurekAlert release, Chicago Booth Review: a dozen experiments with over a thousand people; deep talks with strangers were less awkward and more connecting than predicted; people underestimated others' interest in deep talk.
- Wording safeguard: 'Less awkward than people expected', 'with strangers', brief lab conversations noted.

### Mastroianni, Gilbert, Cooney & Wilson (2021). Do conversations end when people want them to? PNAS 118, e2011809118
- How verified: Searched 2026-11-10; Mastroianni's research page and press release: 932 conversations; conversations almost never ended when both wanted and rarely when even one did; the gap between wished and actual length was about half the conversation; people could not tell when partners wanted to stop. '2%' is press wording only.
- Wording safeguard: 'Almost never', 'about half', no '2%'.

## Claims softened or dropped
- '2% of conversations end when both want': press wording, not in the paper; 'almost never' used.
- '200 ms between turns': secondary summary; narration says 'a fraction of a second' (the on-screen 0.2 SEC is a rough picture of that).
- 'Planning the reply while listening': researchers' interpretation, said as 'researchers think'.
- Tamir & Mitchell: payment amounts differ across sources, so only 'a little money' is said; '80% online' interview claim dropped.
- Newton (1990): dissertation not opened directly; figures agree across multiple summaries; Stanford setting kept, 'student' used.
- Huang (2017): observers of transcripts did not show the effect; 'too many or rude questions can backfire' is said.
- Kardas (2022): brief lab talks with strangers; this limit is said in the narration.
- Derber: 'shift/support response' is a sociologist's concept, not a measured result; said as 'one sociologist called it'.
